understand llms

Everything a large language model does comes out of one boring move, repeated fast.

01

The only thing it does

Predict the next token. Then do it again.

A large language model reads whatever text is in front of it and guesses what comes next. That is the whole job. Not a search. Not a lookup. A guess.

Then it takes its own guess, staples it onto the end of the text, and guesses again. And again. A thousand times in a row, faster than you can read. The essay it writes you was never planned. It was assembled one small piece at a time, and the model never knew where it was going until it got there.

Most people assume it understands the question and then retrieves an answer. It does neither. There is no store of facts to retrieve from, and its confidence tracks how the sentence ought to sound rather than whether the claim is true.

This sounds too simple to produce anything useful. That reaction is correct and also wrong, and the gap between those two things is the interesting part.

02

It does not see words

Everything arrives as chunks, not letters.

Before any of that happens, your text gets chopped into tokens. A token is usually a piece of a word, sometimes a whole common word, sometimes a single character. Every token maps to a number, because the machine underneath is doing arithmetic, not reading.

This sounds like a footnote. It is not. Tokenization quietly explains a whole category of failures that otherwise look like stupidity.

Why it cannot spell strawberry

Ask a model how many r's are in "strawberry" and it may tell you two. Not because it cannot count, but because it never saw the letters. It saw str | aw | berry, three numbers, no spelling. You asked a question about characters to something that perceives the world in chunks.

Same reason arithmetic on long numbers goes sideways, and the same reason a model can be excellent at writing your contract and useless at telling you how many words are in it.

03

A room full of dials

Nobody set them. Failure did.

Between the tokens going in and the guess coming out sits the model itself: an enormous pile of numbers called parameters, or weights. Picture a room of dials, hundreds of billions of them, each one nudging the guess slightly one way or another.

Take a chunk of text from the internet. Hide the next token. Let the model guess. Compare its guess to the real answer. Then nudge every dial a hair in whatever direction would have made the right answer slightly more likely. Do that trillions of times. That is training, and there is no more magic in it than that.

The model is not a library. It is a lossy compression of one, and it cannot tell which parts it lost.

This is also the honest answer to "does it know things." Everything it absorbed got smeared across those dials. Nothing is stored as a fact you could go look up. When the model gets something right, it is reconstructing a plausible answer from a compressed impression, and that process feels identical from the inside whether the reconstruction is accurate or invented. That is what a hallucination is. Not a lie, not a bug, just the same machinery running with worse raw material.

04

Attention

How a word finds out what it means.

Guessing the next token is easy when the answer is a few words back. It is hard when the answer depends on something forty sentences ago, and harder still when one word changes meaning based on a word that comes after it.

The 2017 idea that unlocked all of this is called attention. Before predicting anything, every token gets to look at every other token and ask: which of you actually matter to me right now? The ones that matter get pulled in, and each token quietly updates its own meaning based on the company it keeps.

"Bank" is one thing next to "river" and another next to "loan." No dictionary tells the model that. Attention does, by letting the word absorb its neighbours.

05

Completer to assistant

The personality is a trained document.

Train on the internet and you do not get a helpful assistant. You get something that completes documents. Ask it a question and it might answer, or it might reply with nine more questions, because that is what a page of questions on the internet usually looks like.

Turning that into something you can talk to takes a second phase. Human labellers write thousands of examples of the ideal assistant response, and the model is trained to imitate them. Then a third phase grades responses against each other, so the model learns to steer toward the ones people preferred.

This is the part most people never hear about, and it reframes everything. The "personality" you are talking to is a statistical impression of a helpful human following a style guide. When a model refuses something odd, or hedges, or has a house voice, you are usually seeing that document rather than seeing the model.

06

Thinking out loud

More tokens buys more thought.

A model gets one shot of computation per token. That is a hard ceiling. Ask for the answer to a hard problem in a single token and you are asking it to do all the work in one step, which it cannot do.

So newer models were trained to spend tokens on themselves first. They write out a scratchpad, work the problem in the open, catch their own mistakes, then answer. Reasoning is not a different kind of intelligence bolted on. It is the same next-token loop pointed at its own working, buying itself more steps to think in.

Which is also why "show your work" and "think step by step" made older models measurably smarter. You were manually giving them room they did not know to take.

07

What this buys you

Four rules that fall out of the loop.

Once the loop is in your head the model stops being mysterious and starts being predictable, which is the only thing that matters if you plan to build with it.

01 Context is everything The model has no memory between messages. Its entire world is the text currently in front of it. Every trick that works, retrieval, examples, a good system prompt, pasting the actual document, works for the same reason: you put the answer inside the window it can see.
02 Confidence is not evidence Fluency is the one thing the model was directly optimised for. Correctness came along for the ride. A wrong answer costs it exactly as much effort as a right one, so it will never sound less sure.
03 Show, do not describe Two examples of the output you want will beat two paragraphs describing it. You are steering a pattern matcher. Give it the pattern.
04 Verification is your job Not because the tool is bad, but because nothing in how it was built makes it responsible for being right. That part never got automated, and pretending otherwise is how people get burned.
